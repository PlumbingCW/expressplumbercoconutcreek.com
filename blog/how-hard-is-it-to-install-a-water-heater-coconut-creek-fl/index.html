How Hard Is It to Install a Water Heater in Coconut Creek, FL?

Swapping a tank water heater looks simple on video. In practice it mixes plumbing, electrical or gas work, venting and a heavy tank, and each piece has its own way of going wrong.

The short answer: moderate for a tank swap, hard to do safely

Replacing a tank water heater in the same spot with the same fuel is one of the more doable home plumbing jobs. It is also a job where a small mistake can scald someone, flood a garage or leak gas. That mix is why the honest rating is moderate difficulty and high consequence.

The job breaks into six parts: draining and disconnecting the old unit, moving a tank that can weigh well over a hundred pounds when full, connecting water, connecting gas or power, venting if the heater burns gas, and testing. Each part has a failure mode that does not show up until later.

How hard each part of the job really is

Here is a plain rating for each step, based on the tools and skill involved.

StepDifficultyWhat goes wrong
Drain the old tankEasyA stuck drain valve or sediment can stop the flow
Move the tankModerateWeight and tight doorways cause back injuries and dented walls
Connect water linesModeratePoor soldering or wrong fittings leak slowly behind the tank
Connect power (electric)HardWrong wire size or a loose lug can overheat a connection
Connect gas and vent (gas)HardA gas leak or a backdrafting vent is a safety hazard
Set the relief valve and testModerateA blocked discharge line defeats the safety device

Gas versus electric: what changes in the install

The fuel changes the work more than the tank does.

Electric tank install

  • Needs the right breaker and wire size already in place.
  • No vent pipe, so the tank can go in tighter spaces.
  • The main risk is a loose electrical connection or working on a live circuit.

Gas tank install

  • Needs a gas line, a sediment trap, a shutoff and a flue to the outdoors.
  • Every gas joint must be tested for leaks before the heater runs.
  • The main risk is a gas leak or a flue that does not draw.

Parts people skip: relief valve, expansion tank and drain pan

The temperature and pressure relief valve is the safety device that opens if the tank overheats or overpressurizes. It needs a discharge pipe that runs down to a safe spot and nothing that can block it. Capping that pipe or running it uphill defeats the whole point of the valve.

An expansion tank absorbs the extra volume of water as it heats in a closed system. If your home has a backflow device or pressure-reducing valve on the incoming line, a small expansion tank is often part of a proper installation. A drain pan under the heater in a living space catches a slow leak before it reaches the floor.

These are the parts that separate a clean installation from one that works for a month. They are also the parts a rushed DIY job tends to leave out.

What a professional installation looks like

  1. 1

    Size and plan

    Pick the tank size from how many people live in the home and how many fixtures run at once.

  2. 2

    Shut off and drain

    Turn off the fuel and water, drain the old tank and cut it free.

  3. 3

    Set and connect

    Place the new heater on a stand or pan, connect lines, valves and the expansion tank.

  4. 4

    Fill and test

    Fill the tank before any power or fire, then check every joint, vent and relief line.

When not to do it yourself

Skip the DIY route if the heater is gas and the flue or gas line must change, if the old unit sits in an attic or tight closet, or if you are not sure the electrical supply matches the new tank. Also skip it when the tank is leaking now. A failing tank can burst while you work, so treat it as an emergency and call for water heater replacement.

You may also prefer a tankless unit that mounts on a wall. That changes the gas and venting requirements and the drain and descale routine, and it is covered on our tankless water heater page.

Before you buy a new tank

These checks keep the purchase from turning into a second trip to the store.

  • Measure the space, including height with the flue or relief line attached.
  • Note the fuel, the venting type and the voltage, if electric.
  • Check the date on the old tank, since a heater past its normal life often fails soon after.
  • Ask whether the old unit is leaking or just losing heat, since that decides whether to repair or replace. Our water heater repair page explains the difference.
  • Ask your Building Department whether a permit is needed before work starts.
